Axolotl's mission

The AI that spots breast cancer in time.

Axolotl reads mammograms and shows radiologists which areas to check first. We are starting where the need is greatest: West Africa, where there are too few radiologists to read every mammogram in time.

01

The problem

In Africa, breast cancer kills first because it is found too late.

Breast cancer found early can be treated in the vast majority of cases: in the United States, the five-year relative survival rate is above 99% at a localized stage. If it is found late, once it has spread to distant organs, survival falls to 32%.1 The difference lies in the image, and in how long it takes a specialist to read it.

02

What worked elsewhere

In Sweden, AI already helps radiologists find more breast cancers.

Between 2021 and 2022, the MASAI trial enrolled nearly 106,000 women screened in Sweden. Half had the usual reading by two radiologists. For the other half, reading was supported by AI, and the radiologist kept the final decision.6

+29 %

more cancers detected, with no increase in false alarms.6

−44 %

less reading work for radiologists.6

The final results, published in The Lancet in 2026, show that AI-supported reading does not let more cancers slip through between two screenings, and that fewer of those cancers are aggressive.7

What has to be adapted here

These results come from one country, with one type of mammography machine and one AI system.7 Nothing guarantees they carry over as they are to other machines, other patients and another health system. In Benin, a study in public hospitals concludes that the imaging needed for breast cancer care remains very hard to access, and recommends creating a national mammography screening programme.8 This is where AI can help most: even in Sweden, where breast radiologists are in short supply,9 it cut their reading work by 44%.6

Axolotl is built on MedGemma, an open but general-purpose medical model that is not specialised in mammography. Our method has three steps:

  1. 1. Evaluate

    The starting point

    Test MedGemma as it is on mammograms, to measure what it can already do.

  2. 2. Adapt

    Fine-tuning

    Specialise the model by fine-tuning it, first on public mammograms, then on mammograms from the region.

  3. 3. Re-evaluate

    With our radiologists

    Measure the gain on local mammograms, with our partner radiologists, before any clinical use.

As in MASAI, Axolotl flags and the radiologist decides.

What we need

  • Computeto evaluate MedGemma, fine-tune it and measure the gain.
  • A starting datasetpublic mammograms for the first evaluation, then local mammograms collected with our partners, with authorisation from Benin's data protection authority (APDP) and an ethics committee's approval.
  • Partner radiologiststo annotate mammograms and run the validation.
  • Initial fundingto run the clinical pilot in Benin.

03

Why the name

The axolotl rebuilds what it loses, without growth running out of control.

This small Mexican salamander can regrow a whole leg: bone, muscle, nerves and skin. It also regenerates its tail, spinal cord, heart and parts of its brain.

The growth stays orderly and stops once the limb is complete. Laboratories around the world study it for its regeneration. It is also suspected of resisting cancer, though no one yet understands why.

This resistance is not absolute: tumours have been observed in axolotls, and science is still trying to explain it. Further reading: Lagunas-Rangel, “Are Axolotls Resistant to Cancer? Possible Explanations”, a preprint that has not been peer reviewed.

Our mission is in its name: spot growth that steps out of line early, so the doctor can act in time.

Here, an axolotl leg rebuilds itself. The amber dots that step out of line disappear: a picture of what we want to spot. In the axolotl, a whole leg rebuilds itself, then growth stops on its own.

04

The product

A second look at every mammogram.

Axolotl fits into the radiology department's workflow. Suspicious mammograms move to the top of the list, and the radiologist sees right away where to look.

  1. 1. Import

    The mammogram arrives

    Images are sent in DICOM format, from the hospital's PACS or a single workstation.

  2. 2. Analysis

    The AI flags

    The model scans each image, outlines and measures unusual areas, and ranks mammograms by urgency.

  3. 3. Decision

    The radiologist decides

    They confirm, correct or dismiss each flag. The diagnosis always stays in their hands.

05

Our edge

Why Axolotl, and why now.

An open medical model that we specialise

MedGemma can already read medical images, but it is not specialised in mammography. We fine-tune it on public mammograms, then on regional ones. We do not have to fund years of basic research: every franc invested goes into this specialisation and into clinical validation.

Validation planned on local mammograms

An AI trained in Europe or the United States sees other patients and other machines. We plan to evaluate Axolotl with partner radiologists, on mammograms from the region, during the clinical pilot. This local validation will be a barrier that foreign players do not have.

Built for the field

Axolotl is designed to work with the mammography machines already in place, from teaching hospitals with a PACS to imaging centres that export images from a single workstation.

One regional market

The eight WAEMU countries share a working language, a currency and a regional framework. From Cotonou, we can serve them with one product and one team.

The doctor stays in charge

Every flag is explained: position, size, type of finding. The radiologist judges, and that is what makes the tool acceptable to medical teams.

Protected health data

Mammograms will be encrypted, processed only for the analysis and never sold. Each facility will stay in control of its images.
